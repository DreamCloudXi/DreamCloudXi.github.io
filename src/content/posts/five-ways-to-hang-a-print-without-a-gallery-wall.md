## Lean it

The simplest thing you can do with a framed print is not hang it at all. A wide
shelf, a mantel or the top of a low bookcase will hold a frame leaning back
against the wall, and nothing is committed: you can move it in a second, swap it
seasonally, or stand a second frame in front of it and let them overlap.

Leaning also solves the problem of a wall you are not allowed to drill.

## Hang it low

The received advice is to center a picture at eye level, which in practice means
somewhere around 145cm. That is right in a hallway you walk past and wrong above
a sofa you sit on. Hang to the furniture, not to the room: about 20cm of wall
between the top of the sofa back and the bottom of the frame reads as one
composition rather than two objects.

## Use the narrow wall

The strip of wall between a door and a corner, the return beside a window, the
panel at the end of a landing — these are the pieces of a room nobody plans for,
and a single tall print turns them from an awkward gap into something deliberate.

## Make a pair, not a grid

Two prints of the same size, hung with a hand's width between them, do most of
what a gallery wall does and take a tenth of the decisions. If you want a third,
put it somewhere else in the room rather than in the line.

## Put it where you will actually look at it

The best-lit wall in the house is often the one facing the window, which is also
the one that will fade a print fastest. Between the two, choose the wall you pass
every day and glaze the frame with something UV-filtering. A print you see is
worth more than a print you preserve.
